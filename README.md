## The entry

> readrift — reaching the bottom of a page and realizing you absorbed none of it.

79 characters, one line.

## Why it wins

1. **A genuinely new word, defined in one clear line.** *readrift* appears in no dictionary. The whole entry is a single line of 79 characters: the word, then its definition, nothing else.
2. **A real gap, recognised at once.** Every reader has done this — eyes travelling down the page while the mind is elsewhere, arriving at the bottom with nothing retained. English has loose phrases for it ("zoning out", "reading without taking it in") but no single word.
3. **Exact and self-contained.** The definition lands in one reading: the bottom of the page, the realisation, the nothing absorbed. No second sentence or example is needed.
4. **Well formed and easy to live with.** Built from two plain English roots, *read* + *drift*, and it echoes *adrift* — your attention is adrift while your eyes keep reading. Sayable (REED-drift), spellable, memorable.
5. **Fresh and surprising.** The pairing is new, yet the moment it is read it feels like a word English should already have had — the kind of word a reader immediately wants to use ("Sorry, total readrift — what did that page say?").

## How it was chosen

*readrift* was picked from 25 candidate coinages, each scored 1–5 against the five judging criteria above. The nearest runners-up were *namefade* (a name evaporating seconds after an introduction), *draftghost* (a long message typed, then deleted unsent), *doorjog* (the awkward half-jog when someone holds a door open from too far away) and *pocketpat* (the automatic pat-down for keys, phone and wallet before leaving). *readrift* was the only candidate scoring 5/5 on the first four criteria: new, universal, exact, and well formed.
